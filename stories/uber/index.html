How this was made

Every number comes from Uber's quarterly earnings releases, which it files with the SEC as Exhibit 99.1 to a Form 8-K, plus its April 2019 IPO prospectus for the first half of 2018. I read each release and copied its tables line by line into data/raw/, naming the release each line came from. pipeline/build_uber.py then checks them and writes data/quarters.csv, which is the only file this page reads.

The checks: within each table the business lines add up to the printed total; most quarters appear in two releases (their own and the next year's), and the two copies must match; and each quarter's total revenue must match the figure in Uber's machine-readable SEC filings. … The full log is in data/build_log.txt.

“Rides” is Uber's Mobility segment and “delivery” its Delivery segment; Uber renamed them from Rides and Eats in 2020. The grey band is everything else: Freight, plus small businesses Uber later sold or closed. Before 2020 the releases do not split Freight out, so the grey band then is the total minus rides and delivery.

The $20 ride and order scale Uber's own totals for …: revenue ÷ gross bookings, and segment profit ÷ gross bookings, for each business line, times $20. They are averages over every trip in the quarter, not a receipt for any one trip. Segment profit is Uber's “segment operating income”, which leaves out head-office costs and shared technology spending (… that quarter), so Uber as a whole keeps less than the two figures suggest. Uber switched profit measures in late 2025. The profit chart uses its older measure, segment adjusted EBITDA, until …, and the newer one, dotted, after that. The newer measure runs lower, as the overlap in the chart shows.

Revenue before the fourth quarter of 2019 is left out of the revenue chart. Uber changed how it presents some payments to drivers in late 2020 and restated 2019 and 2020, but the releases only restate back to the fourth quarter of 2019. Gross bookings were not affected.

Sources

    What is left out

    • Gross bookings by region. Uber's releases give only revenue by region, and only through mid-2023. Revenue by region is distorted by the 2022 British change, which moved about $1 billion a quarter into Europe's revenue.
    • City counts. Uber's prospectus counts “over 700 cities” in 2018 and its 2019 annual report counts about 10,000, which are clearly different definitions, so the two aren't compared here.
    • How the driver's share splits from taxes, tolls and fees. Uber doesn't break that out each quarter.
    • Figures for 2014 to 2017, which Uber has published only as yearly totals.
